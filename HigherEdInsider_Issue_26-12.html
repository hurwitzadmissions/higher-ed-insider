1

Federal Court Blocks Admissions Data Mandate — For Now

The week's biggest story: a coalition of 17 state attorneys general — led by Massachusetts and including New York, California, Illinois, and others — filed suit in federal court in Boston seeking to block a sweeping Trump administration mandate requiring colleges to submit seven years of detailed admissions data, broken down by race, gender, test scores, GPA, family income, and financial aid. The mandate, known as the Admissions and Consumer Transparency Supplement (ACTS), was added to the longstanding federal IPEDS reporting system and required initial compliance by March 18. A federal judge granted a temporary restraining order blocking enforcement while the case proceeds.

The administration framed the data collection as a post-affirmative action compliance tool, following the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling ending race-conscious admissions. The states argue the mandate was rushed through without proper rulemaking, imposes impossible burdens on institutions, and threatens student privacy. Institutions that fail to comply face potential fines exceeding $71,000 per violation and risk losing federal funding.

Why it matters for your family
This legal battle will define how much transparency — and how much federal oversight — surrounds selective college admissions in the coming years. The outcome will affect how colleges document and discuss their admissions processes, and may increase pressure on institutions to further deprioritize holistic review in favor of quantifiable metrics.
→ Inside Higher Ed  ·  Higher Ed Dive  ·  McGuireWoods Legal Alert  ·  WBUR
2

NIH Grant Funding Is 74% Below Historical Averages — With No Recovery in Sight

A new analysis published this week by Johns Hopkins researchers found that as of March 3, the NIH had issued 74% fewer competitive new grant awards than the average for the same period over the prior four fiscal years. A separate nationwide survey of nearly 1,000 NIH-funded researchers by STAT News — published today — describes labs closing, junior faculty losing positions, and early-career scientists abandoning research careers entirely. One researcher described the situation as "the Titanic hitting the iceberg": the music is still playing, the ship is sinking.

Congress has largely rejected the administration's proposed 40% cut to NIH's overall budget, and a federal court has blocked the attempted cap on research overhead costs. But grant award rates remain far below normal, and the human cost is becoming visible: layoffs, paused clinical trials, and a generation of young scientists being driven out of the field. The proposed FY 2026 budget would reduce NIH support to inflation-adjusted levels not seen in over 25 years.

Why it matters for your family
Students targeting STEM fields at research universities should understand that the federal science funding environment is in genuine distress. Graduate program capacity, research mentorship opportunities, and the academic job market in the sciences are all contracting. This is a significant factor in college and graduate school planning for students interested in biomedical research, public health, and related fields.
→ STAT News  ·  Nature  ·  U.S. News & World Report
3

International Student Enrollment Down 17% — Visa Issuances Down 36%

New data confirmed this week that new international student enrollment at U.S. colleges fell 17% in fall 2025 compared to the prior year — and a revised analysis shows that student visa issuances actually dropped by 36%, worse than earlier estimates. The decline is attributed to visa processing suspensions, travel bans affecting nearly 20 countries, expanded social media vetting requirements, and a climate of uncertainty for international students. SEVIS record terminations — affecting students' legal status without warning — have created persistent anxiety on campuses nationwide.

The financial stakes are significant. International students contribute an estimated $44 billion annually to the U.S. economy. NAFSA projects that a sustained enrollment decline could cost universities $7 billion in revenue and eliminate 60,000 jobs. Some institutions, including DePaul University in Chicago, have already initiated budget cuts and layoffs in response. Roughly 40% of Ph.D. students in STEM fields are foreign-born, a group that drives a substantial portion of U.S. research innovation.

Why it matters for your family
The decline in international enrollment is already affecting graduate program composition, research team capacity, and campus culture at many universities. Institutions that relied heavily on international tuition revenue are tightening budgets, which can affect financial aid availability for domestic students and the resources available for campus services.
→ EdSource  ·  Higher Ed Dive  ·  Brookings Institution  ·  Inside Higher Ed
4

Common App Reports Black Students Are Fastest-Growing Applicant Group

A mid-season report from Common App released this week found that first-year applicants from underrepresented racial and ethnic minority groups grew 5% compared to the same point in the 2024-25 cycle. Black and African American applicants grew at the fastest rate — up 8% year over year — continuing a trend that has persisted through nearly three full admissions cycles following the Supreme Court's 2023 affirmative action ruling. First-generation applicants were up 7%, and applicants from below-median income ZIP codes grew 8%.

Common App researchers noted "no meaningful deviations from pre-existing trends" in the racial makeup of the applicant pool since the ruling. However, separate Urban Institute data reveals a divergence: while Black students are applying at increasing rates, the share of Black students being admitted at highly selective universities has declined — suggesting the ruling's effects are concentrated in the admissions decision stage, not the application stage.

Why it matters for your family
Application volume is increasing broadly, especially to selective institutions. The pool of competitive applicants continues to grow, making positioning and narrative strategy more important — not less — regardless of a student's background. The divergence between application rates and admission rates at elite universities reinforces that applying is only the first step; the quality and clarity of the application itself is what determines outcomes.
→ Common App  ·  Higher Ed Dive  ·  Urban Institute
5

Department of Education Warns Two Major Accreditors Over DEI Standards

The Department of Education's Under Secretary Nicholas Kent sent formal letters this week to the Middle States Commission on Higher Education and the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education, warning both that DEI-related provisions in their accreditation standards conflict with federal law. Both accreditors had their federal recognition renewed — but the letters signal that the administration intends to use the accreditation system as a lever for enforcing its anti-DEI agenda across the full range of higher education institutions.

Middle States is the regional accreditor for colleges and universities in the mid-Atlantic region, including Delaware, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, and Washington D.C. Its standards affect hundreds of institutions that families in the Northeast apply to every year.

Why it matters for your family
Accreditation is the mechanism by which federal financial aid — including Pell Grants and student loans — flows to colleges. When the federal government pressures accreditors, it shapes what institutions can and cannot require or encourage in their programs. Students applying to schools in the Middle States region should be aware that their institutions are operating in a period of regulatory pressure that may affect campus culture, programming, and leadership decisions.
→ Inside Higher Ed  ·  Higher Ed Dive
6

The Department of Education Continues Its Structural Contraction

Multiple stories this week tracked the ongoing reduction of the Department of Education. As of March 2026, the department has shrunk from approximately 4,100 employees to around 2,800 — a roughly 40% reduction — following a March 2025 executive order directing the agency to begin winding down non-essential functions. Several program areas have been transferred to other agencies: career and technical education to the Department of Labor, school safety grants to Health and Human Services, and foreign funding oversight to the Department of State.

New legislation proposed this week by the Department would also ease the pathway for struggling institutions to merge — a response to the accelerating financial pressures facing smaller and mid-size colleges. The Department of Labor is now soliciting applications for TRIO grant programs on Education's behalf — the latest example of core higher education functions migrating out of their traditional home agency.

Why it matters for your family
A smaller, less staffed Department of Education means slower processing of FAFSA applications, less consistent compliance oversight, and reduced support for students navigating the federal aid system. Families should plan for longer timelines and less predictability in federal financial aid administration in the 2026-27 cycle and beyond.
→ Inside Higher Ed  ·  ACE Headlines  ·  AcademicJobs.com
7

Student Mental Health Remains the Top Concern of College Presidents

A survey cited in multiple publications this week found that 70% of college presidents identify student mental health as their single greatest institutional challenge — yet only 35% of institutions have comprehensive mental health strategies that include prevention alongside treatment. Campus counseling center utilization has grown five times faster than enrollment over the past decade. Waitlists of several weeks remain common at many universities, and a new report this week described the gap between student demand and institutional capacity as widening, not narrowing, despite increased investment.

Colleges are increasingly moving toward peer-support models, stepped-care frameworks, and telehealth partnerships to extend capacity. The fundamental challenge: the U.S. Health Resources and Services Administration projects that demand for mental health professionals will exceed supply by 250,000 full-time providers — making hiring alone an insufficient solution for any institution.

Why it matters for your family
When evaluating colleges, mental health infrastructure deserves the same scrutiny as academic programs. Families should ask specific questions during campus visits: What is the average wait time for a first counseling appointment? What crisis resources are available after hours? What is the student-to-counselor ratio? These questions reveal institutional commitment more clearly than brochure language.
→ The Hill  ·  Psychology Today  ·  American Council on Education
8

Iowa Advances Bills That Would Dramatically Reshape Public University Operations

Iowa's state legislature advanced multiple bills this week that would significantly restructure how the state's public universities can operate — including measures targeting curriculum oversight, faculty governance, and institutional hiring practices. The bills are part of a broader wave of state-level higher education legislation. Iowa joins Florida, Texas, Indiana, and Missouri in advancing legislation that expands legislative oversight of university operations and restricts certain academic programs and administrative practices.

A separate bill advancing in the Florida House would cap nonresident students at no more than 5% of first-time, full-time fall enrollment at certain public institutions — one of the most aggressive enrollment-restriction proposals yet seen at the state level.

Why it matters for your family
State-level legislation increasingly shapes what happens on public university campuses — from what programs exist to how faculty are hired and evaluated. Families considering public flagship universities in states with active legislative intervention in higher education should factor this climate into their college list decisions and campus visit conversations.
→ Higher Ed Dive  ·  Chronicle of Higher Education
9

Ohio State Names Its Next President — From Within

Ohio State University's Board of Trustees this week unanimously appointed provost Walter "Ted" Carter, Jr. as the university's permanent president, bypassing an extended national search process following the abrupt resignation of his predecessor. The appointment reflects a growing trend among large public universities toward internal succession as a stability strategy during a turbulent period for higher education governance.

Ohio State is one of the nation's largest and most selective public universities, with significant merit scholarship offerings and a competitive honors college program.

Why it matters for your family
Presidential leadership at large public universities directly affects admissions philosophy, financial aid strategy, and institutional priorities. Families with students targeting Ohio State should monitor how the new administration approaches selectivity, merit scholarships, and its relationship with the Ohio state legislature in the months ahead.
→ Associated Press  ·  Inside Higher Ed
A Note From Dr. Hurwitz

This week's news is unusually consequential for families with college-bound students. Three threads in particular are worth holding together: the admissions data mandate lawsuit, the shift in admissions demographics following the end of affirmative action, and the continuing contraction of the Department of Education.

The admissions landscape is not becoming simpler — it is becoming more complex and more politically charged. The institutions your student is applying to are navigating legal, regulatory, and financial pressures that were not present three years ago. In that environment, the quality and clarity of a student's application matters more, not less. Strategy, narrative coherence, and thoughtful positioning are not luxuries — they are the appropriate response to a process that has grown genuinely more difficult to predict.
