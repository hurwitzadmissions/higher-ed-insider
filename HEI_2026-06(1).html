TOPIC 01Federal Policy / Dept. of Education

McMahon Signals Shift Away from Higher Education — What It Means for Institutions

Secretary McMahon told Breitbart the new year is a chance to 'shift a little bit away from higher education.' For families, this is a planning signal, not a reassurance.

Education Secretary Linda McMahon publicly stated that 2026 will see the administration shift its focus away from higher education and toward K–12, school choice, and 'returning education to the states.' Her priorities for the year: literacy, school choice, and dismantling the federal Department of Education's Washington bureaucracy.

The statement should not be read as relief for universities. The mechanisms of federal pressure on higher education — Title VI investigations, funding freezes, accreditation pressure, and DEI enforcement — are largely delegated and self-executing at this point. The administration has also transferred education programs via interagency agreements to the Departments of Labor and HHS, reducing the visible footprint of the Education Department while maintaining leverage.

For families, the practical question is: what does reduced federal attention to higher education mean for FAFSA processing, student aid, and institutional stability? The week's other news suggests the answer is: complexity, not relief.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Secretary McMahon's statement is less a policy change than a reallocation of political attention. The structural threats to higher education — funding uncertainty, accreditation pressure, Title VI investigations — are already embedded in operating procedures and ongoing litigation. They do not require ongoing executive attention to continue. Families should not interpret this signal as a normalization of the higher education funding environment.

Key Concerns

  • Program transfers to other agencies create processing delays and compliance confusion

TOPIC 02Research Funding / NIH

NIH Reverses Hundreds of Rejected Grants Under Court Order — But the Chilling Effect Remains

Federal courts forced NIH to reconsider grants it shelved or denied in 2025. The reversals are real — but the damage to the research pipeline is not undone.

The National Institutes of Health agreed in late December 2025 court filings to reconsider hundreds of grant applications it had previously rejected or shelved as part of the administration's effort to eliminate DEI and politically disfavored research. The agreements set a series of deadlines through July 31, 2026, by which NIH must decide on these applications.

The reversals followed a year in which NIH awarded 3,500 fewer competitive grants than historical averages, with the steepest declines at institutes focused on minority health, nursing, human genome, alcohol abuse and alcoholism, and mental health — all areas the administration characterized as 'woke.' Researchers described the environment as demoralizing and destabilizing.

The practical consequence for families: the research university ecosystem that underlies much of elite higher education is under sustained stress. Programs in public health, social sciences, and humanities at research universities have been restructured, reduced, or eliminated. The court reversals are a partial correction, not a restoration.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
NIH reversals are good news for researchers whose grants were improperly denied. But they do not restore the labs that closed, the postdocs who left, or the graduate programs that were cut during the funding freeze. Families applying to research-intensive universities should ask specifically about the stability of programs in their intended field — particularly in public health, social sciences, and the humanities — and whether recent doctoral program cuts affect their intended area of study.
Potential Benefits
  • Court victories are establishing precedent limiting arbitrary grant termination
  • Congress preserved NIH and NSF budgets at roughly current levels despite White House cuts
  • Some previously terminated grants are being reinstated following litigation
Key Concerns
  • 3,500 fewer grants than historical average in 2025 — pipeline damage will take years to reverse
  • 16 of 27 NIH institutes lacked permanent directors as of mid-February
  • Researchers abroad are actively being recruited from U.S. institutions; brain drain accelerating
Key Articles This Week

  • The New York Times — NIH grant cuts data analysis
  • Times Higher Education — 'US Science Budgets Have Been Spared. So Why Is No One Celebrating?' (March 2026)
✓ Action Checklist

  • Ask whether doctoral programs in your intended field have been cut or restructured
  • Check if your target research university has settled or is litigating with the administration
→ Action Steps
  1. For STEM-focused applicants: look up your target university's NIH grant history on NIH Reporter
  2. Ask during campus visits: how has your department been affected by federal research funding changes?
  3. Research which universities have the most diversified funding sources — philanthropy, industry, state — beyond federal grants
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Has your institution had any federal research grants terminated in the past 12 months?'
  • 'Are any programs in my intended field of study at risk due to funding changes?'

TOPIC 03International Enrollment / Revenue

The International Student Crisis: $6.2B Revenue Gap and What Fills It

A 41% decline in international student web searches and 57% of colleges reporting enrollment drops point to a revenue reckoning — with direct consequences for domestic financial aid.

The data is stark: a 41% decline in year-over-year web visits to international student recruitment services, a preliminary survey showing 57% of colleges reporting a decline in international enrollment in fall 2025, and a projected $6.2 billion revenue loss across U.S. postgraduate programs by fall 2026. These are not projections built on worst-case assumptions — they are the leading indicators of an already-in-progress enrollment collapse.

The mechanism matters for families. International students — particularly graduate students in STEM fields — have historically paid full or near-full tuition while receiving minimal institutional aid. That revenue cross-subsidized domestic financial aid budgets at many universities. As international enrollment drops, institutions must choose: cut programs, reduce aid, or find replacement revenue from domestic full-payers.

NAFSA: Association of International Educators is entering 2026 expecting a 'sharp drop' in graduate enrollment. Visa revocations exceeded 8,000 in 2025. Travel bans affect 39 countries. The Trump administration also proposed capping student visas at four years, disproportionately affecting graduate students in multi-year programs.

47% of grad students and 58% of postdocs in science/engineering are international. Among the most exposed institutions to visa and enrollment policy changes.
UC System
Over $4.6B in federal R&D spending with heavy international graduate enrollment. California's parallel state budget cuts compound the pressure.
Harvard
Domestic undergrad pipeline strong; international graduate programs under pressure from both enrollment decline and visa policy.
Small Liberal Arts Colleges
Lower exposure to international graduate enrollment; more insulated from this specific pressure than research universities.
Research universities with heavy international graduate enrollment in STEM are most exposed to this revenue disruption. For families choosing between research universities and liberal arts colleges, this is now a relevant financial stability factor.
Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The international enrollment story is the most underappreciated financial variable in family college planning right now. If a university's financial model was built on 20–30% international graduate enrollment and that number drops 15–19%, the institution faces a real budget choice. The most vulnerable institutions are those with high international enrollment concentration in STEM graduate programs — and they are often the same institutions families associate with the strongest graduate outcomes in those fields. Ask direct questions about financial aid budget stability.
Potential Benefits
  • Enrollment pressure may increase domestic merit aid competition among institutions
  • Some universities are expanding recruitment in underrepresented domestic markets to compensate
  • Undergraduate enrollment drops are smaller than graduate — the domestic undergraduate market remains relatively stable
Key Concerns
  • $6.2B projected revenue loss by fall 2026 — impossible to replace with domestic enrollment alone
  • 19% fewer new international master's students in 2025, with more drops expected in 2026
  • Graduate programs in CS, engineering, and public health most exposed — staffing and course availability affected

TOPIC 04Research Workforce / Brain Drain

American Researchers Are Leaving — Foreign Universities Are Recruiting Them Actively

As U.S. federal grant cancellations mount, European, Canadian, and Asian universities have launched explicit campaigns to recruit displaced American researchers. The consequences for U.S. higher education could be generational.

Higher Ed Dive reported in January 2026 that foreign institutions — particularly in Europe, Canada, and China — are ramping up recruitment of American researchers as the Trump administration cancels federal grants en masse. By March 2025, about three-quarters of more than 1,600 scientists surveyed said they were considering leaving the U.S. due to research funding disruptions, according to a poll in the journal Nature.

The brain drain has specific institutional and geographic concentration. Research universities under federal pressure — Harvard, Columbia, Johns Hopkins, Northwestern — are losing researchers who cannot sustain their work under the current funding environment. The loss is not just to institutions; it is to the graduate and doctoral students who trained under these researchers.

The U.S. News report noted that several universities including Brown were forced to cancel doctoral programs in humanities and social sciences for 2026–27 as a result of funding cuts. These are not temporary pauses — they are structural reductions in graduate education capacity.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The brain drain story matters to families for a concrete reason: the quality of graduate mentorship and undergraduate research opportunities at research universities is directly tied to the faculty they retain. If the researchers most dependent on federal grants are the ones most likely to leave, the universities that lose them will offer a different educational experience than their rankings suggest. For families considering research universities, this is worth asking about directly.
Potential Benefits
  • Many leading researchers remain committed to their institutions and students despite funding pressures
  • Some universities are responding with targeted philanthropy and industry partnerships to retain key researchers
  • Courts have provided meaningful protection for some research programs through injunctions
Key Concerns
  • Three-quarters of surveyed scientists considering leaving the U.S. is a historic signal
  • Doctoral program cancellations at Brown and others represent permanent reductions in graduate education capacity
  • Young researchers choosing career paths are being deterred from the sciences — long-term pipeline damage
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Will Higher Education Researchers Leave for Opportunities Abroad?' (Jan. 5, 2026)
  • Nature — Survey of 1,600+ federally funded researchers on career intentions (March 2025)
  • U.S. News & World Report — 'Biggest Developments in Higher Education Policy in 2025' (Jan. 2, 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Research whether key faculty in your intended field at target schools have recently left or announced departures
  • Check for doctoral program changes in your area — cuts at graduate level often precede undergraduate program changes
  • For pre-med, pre-PhD, and research-track students: ask specifically about undergraduate research opportunities
→ Action Steps
  1. Search your target school's news page for 'faculty departure' or 'program restructuring' in the past 12 months
  2. Ask admissions what percentage of undergraduates conduct faculty research — this signals research culture vitality
  3. Look at peer-reviewed publication output from faculty in your intended department for the last 2 years
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Has your institution lost any significant faculty in the past year due to funding pressures?'
  • 'Are undergraduate research opportunities in my intended field currently available and fully funded?'
  • 'Have any doctoral programs been suspended or restructured in the past 12 months?'

TOPIC 05AI in Education / Federal Policy

The Education Department's $169M AI Investment: What It Signals for Admissions and Learning

The Dept. of Education's January 2026 FIPSE grants fund AI for 'responsible use' in teaching, civil discourse, accreditation reform, and short-term credentials. The strategic direction tells families a great deal.

The U.S. Department of Education awarded $169 million from the Fund for the Improvement of Postsecondary Education (FIPSE) in January 2026 for AI-related initiatives in four areas: responsible AI use in teaching and learning, fostering civil discourse on campuses, reforming higher education accreditation, and building capacity for high-quality short-term programs.

The alignment is deliberate: the administration is using federal grant money to advance its institutional reform agenda through AI adoption. 'Civil discourse' grants signal ongoing pressure on campus speech and protest environments. Accreditation reform grants advance the administration's effort to open the accreditation market to new, politically aligned accreditors. Short-term credential grants advance a workforce-over-liberal-arts framing.

For families, the accreditation reform piece is the most consequential. The administration launched the Accreditation, Innovation, and Modernization (AIM) committee in January 2026 with a mandate to simplify accreditor recognition, ban DEI standards, and focus on workforce outcomes. New accreditors aligned with conservative state governments in Florida, Texas, Georgia, and others are positioning to enter the market.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Accreditation reform sounds technical but is practically significant: it determines whether a degree is eligible for federal financial aid. If the AIM committee creates pathways for new accreditors that emphasize workforce credentials over liberal arts, the value and transferability of degrees from newly-accredited institutions could be very different from what families expect. For now, the major existing accreditors cover the institutions most families are considering. But this is a space to monitor, especially for families evaluating newer or less-traditional programs.
Potential Benefits
  • AI investment in teaching can improve personalized learning and student support services
  • Short-term credential investment creates new options for workforce-focused students
  • Congress preserved funding for traditional student aid programs despite these policy shifts
Key Concerns
  • Accreditation reform aimed at 'banning DEI standards' may reduce attention to equity in access and outcomes
  • New accreditors may not provide the quality assurance that makes degrees valuable to employers
  • AI implementation in admissions raises ongoing equity concerns for first-gen and non-traditional students
Key Articles This Week
  • U.S. Department of Education — FIPSE Grant Announcement (Jan. 5, 2026)
  • Inside Higher Ed — 'ED Panel Kicks Off Accountability Talks' (Jan. 6, 2026)
  • Educational Technology and Change Journal — 'AI as a Pillar of Institutional Strategy' (Jan. 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Verify that every institution on your list is accredited by one of the seven major regional accreditors
  • Ask about accreditation stability if you are considering newer or for-profit programs
  • For programs using AI-assisted advising or admissions review: ask how the school ensures equity in algorithmic processes
→ Action Steps
  1. Check accreditation status at ope.ed.gov for any institution you are considering
  2. Research whether your target institution has entered any agreements related to accreditation reform
  3. Ask admissions about AI use in application review — the answer reveals institutional transparency
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Is your institution's accreditor among the established major regional accreditors?'
  • 'Has your accreditation status been subject to any review or challenge in the past two years?'
  • 'How does your institution use AI in advising, placement, or admissions?'

TOPIC 06Graduate Education / Doctoral Programs

Doctoral Program Cuts Ripple Through the University: What Families Need to Know

Brown, and others have canceled doctoral programs for 2026–27. Short-term credential enrollment is up 14.7%. The shape of American graduate education is changing — and undergraduate families should pay attention.

Several universities — including Brown University — were forced to cancel doctoral programs, primarily in humanities and social sciences, for the 2026–27 academic year as a result of federal funding cuts. The cancellations are concentrated in fields that were most targeted by the administration's DEI and 'woke research' grant terminations: education, sociology, ethnic studies, public health, and environmental studies.

Against this backdrop, short-term credential enrollment has risen 14.7% since 2023, outpacing every other credential type by at least 6.5 percentage points. Over 30 states have invested a combined $8.1 billion in short-term workforce credentials in 2025. The framing from the administration and from many state governments is explicit: workforce training over traditional liberal arts graduate education.

For undergraduate families, this matters in two ways: first, the graduate program cuts reduce the prestige and research depth of universities in affected fields; second, the credential expansion opens genuinely new pathways that may be more cost-effective and more directly employable in specific industries.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The credential vs. degree debate is not abstract for families of high schoolers who will be making college choices in the next two to four years. The administration's policy direction is toward workforce credentials and short-term programs. The quality and employment value of these credentials varies enormously. My advice: if a student is considering a traditional four-year path at a research university, the institutions most committed to that model are the ones most actively fighting to preserve it. That institutional commitment matters for long-term academic quality.
Potential Benefits
  • Short-term credentials offer faster, cheaper paths to employment in specific fields
  • 14.7% growth in credential enrollment suggests strong market demand and employer interest
  • Some state-backed credential programs have strong industry partnerships and employment outcomes
Key Concerns
  • Doctoral program cancellations reduce graduate mentorship and research depth at affected universities
  • Employer recognition of non-degree credentials is inconsistent and field-dependent
  • Liberal arts programs face sustained political and financial pressure that may continue reducing program offerings
Key Articles This Week
  • U.S. News & World Report — Doctoral Program Cancellations at Brown (Jan. 2, 2026)
  • University Business — 'Here Are 5 Higher Education Predictions for 2026' (Jan. 2, 2026)
  • Lumina Foundation — Short-term Credential Enrollment and State Investment Data
✓ Action Checklist
  • For graduate school-bound students: check current status of doctoral programs at target schools in your field
  • Research employer recognition of credential programs in your intended industry before committing
  • Ask research universities: have any graduate programs in my intended field been reduced or eliminated in the past year?
→ Action Steps
  1. Visit each target school's graduate school page and look for news about program changes in the past 12 months
  2. For credential programs: ask for employment outcome data and employer partnership lists before enrolling
  3. Research which professional associations and employers in your field recognize and prefer traditional degrees vs. credentials
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Have any doctoral programs in my intended field been suspended or eliminated recently?'
  • 'What is your institution's commitment to maintaining the traditional four-year liberal arts program?'
  • 'For credential programs: what is the employment rate within 6 months of completion?'

Guidance Counselor Corner

What Every Counselor Should Know and Share This Week

Six areas for counselors this week. The McMahon statement is not a policy change but a political signal — help families understand that federal enforcement mechanisms on higher education are already embedded and self-executing. The most important question families can ask any school is whether it has an active federal investigation or funding dispute, and what the resolution status is.

The NIH research funding story directly affects the graduate mentorship quality at research universities. Counselors advising research-track students should encourage those students to ask specifically about faculty retention and undergraduate research availability in their intended fields. The brain drain story is real and concentrated in exactly the fields where research-track students are most interested.

The international enrollment story is critical for counselors advising students where cost is a variable. The cross-subsidy mechanism — international tuition revenue funding domestic aid — is under stress at exactly the institutions that offer the most generous need-based aid. Encourage families to ask financial aid offices directly whether aid budgets are stable regardless of enrollment revenue changes.

Your Action Guide — Edition 2026-06

Dr. Hurwitz's Analysis · January 5–9, 2026

The first week of January 2026 produces six specific action items. The landscape is changing fast — here is how to keep up.

1
Verify your target schools' federal investigation status
Active investigations and funding disputes are now a legitimate selection factor. Ask admissions directly. Schools settled with the administration have different campus environments than schools still litigating.
2
Ask about financial aid budget stability — specifically
International enrollment drops threaten the cross-subsidy that funds domestic aid. 'Is your aid budget stable regardless of enrollment changes?' is a direct and reasonable question.
3
Research doctoral program changes at research universities
Cancellations at Brown and others are harbingers. For research-track students, check program status in your intended field before targeting a school.
4
Check the accreditation status of every school on your list
AIM committee reforms are underway. For now, the seven major regional accreditors are stable. Verify this for any non-traditional program you are considering.
5
Treat international enrollment percentage as a financial indicator
Schools with high international graduate enrollment face higher revenue risk. Ask for financial aid budget stability commitments in writing.
6
Start demonstrated interest activities now — not in the fall
AI-driven admissions systems already track your engagement. Every campus visit registration and admissions email you send now creates a data record that matters.

Dr. Hurwitz's Bottom Line: The higher education landscape in January 2026 is defined by structural instability that is not visible in rankings or acceptance rates. The families who will navigate this most successfully are the ones who ask specific, direct questions — about funding, about program stability, about financial aid security — rather than relying on institutional reputation alone.
