TOPIC 01Student Loans / Financial Aid

Four in Ten Borrowers Can't Qualify for Private Loans — and Families Don't Know Until April

Inside Higher Ed: Researchers found that four in ten borrowers have credit scores too low for private loans, raising concerns that families are building college plans around borrowing capacity they don't have.

Inside Higher Ed reported on research finding that four in ten student loan borrowers have credit scores too low to qualify for private loans — a finding with enormous practical consequences for the college planning process. Private loans are how many families expect to bridge the gap between institutional aid and the full cost of attendance. The discovery that they don't qualify typically happens in April of senior year, after emotional and logistical commitments have already been made.

The problem is structural: families use net price calculators, receive institutional aid packages, and calculate an expected gap. They then assume a private loan will fill the gap. The step of verifying their private loan qualification status — which takes a phone call or online inquiry — is almost never completed during the planning phase. By the time the family discovers they don't qualify, deposit deadlines are past.

This is distinct from the Parent PLUS loan question. Parent PLUS loans have looser credit standards than private loans and are available to most parents. But Parent PLUS loans carry higher interest rates than private loans, and their repayment is not income-based — a significant financial planning difference for families managing tight budgets.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
This is the most preventable financial planning failure I see consistently. The fix takes 20 minutes: have parents pre-qualify for private loans while still in the junior year planning phase, before any school is on a finalized list. If they don't qualify, the list should be built around schools where aid packages don't require private loan supplementation. The 44% statistic in our Edition 2026-10 data and this week's '4 in 10 borrowers' data point to the same systemic gap.
Potential Benefits
  • Parent PLUS loans remain available as a federal backstop for families who don't qualify for private loans
  • Some institutions offer institutional payment plans that reduce the need for external borrowing
  • Early discovery of private loan ineligibility creates time for list adjustment
Key Concerns
  • Discovery typically happens in April of senior year — after emotional and logistical commitments are made
  • Parent PLUS rates (currently 8%+) are significantly higher than private loan rates for qualified borrowers
  • Families with poor credit history may have no private borrowing option at all, limiting choices to fully-aided schools
Key Articles This Week
  • Inside Higher Ed — 'ED Panel Kicks Off Accountability Talks: Four in Ten Borrowers Have Credit Scores Too Low for Private Loans' (Jan. 6, 2026)
  • College Investor — College Report: Education, Money, and Campus News (Feb. 2026)
  • NASFAA — Student Loan Qualification Research
✓ Action Checklist
  • Have parents pre-qualify for private loans during junior year — not after acceptances arrive
  • Compare Parent PLUS loan rates against private loan rates before deciding which to use
  • Build the college list around schools where net price calculations don't require private loan supplementation
→ Action Steps
  1. Use Sallie Mae, College Ave, or similar lenders' pre-qualification tools to check eligibility before it matters
  2. If private loan qualification is uncertain, build a list with at least three schools where the net price is fully covered by federal and institutional aid
  3. Ask each target school's financial aid office: what is your average student loan debt at graduation? High averages often signal that students are filling gaps with private borrowing.
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'What is the average student loan debt for your graduates — and what percentage borrowed private loans?'
  • 'Does your institution offer a payment plan that reduces or eliminates the need for private loans?'
  • 'If our expected private loan qualification changes, does your aid package have a contingency mechanism?'

TOPIC 02Community College / Access

Free Community College Programs Driving Real Enrollment Gains: What the Numbers Show

Community college campuses saw an average increase of 623 students due to free tuition programs. Statewide re-enrollment initiatives in New Jersey, California, and others are proving scalable. The transfer pathway is being rebuilt.

Higher Ed Dive reported that community college campuses participating in free tuition programs saw an average enrollment increase of 623 students — a meaningful gain for institutions whose financial models depend on enrollment volume. New Jersey has re-enrolled over 13,000 students across 22 participating colleges and universities through statewide stop-out recovery initiatives. Parallel efforts in California, Nebraska, Illinois, and Minnesota are showing similar results.

The re-enrollment initiatives are largely targeting 'stop-out' students — adults who started college but didn't finish, often with a year or more of existing credit. These students have demonstrable academic capability and practical life experience; they also represent a direct solution to labor shortages in fields requiring postsecondary credentials without four-year degrees.

For families of current high school students, the community college pipeline story has a different implication: the transfer pathway is being rebuilt with significant state investment and institutional attention. Students who enter community college with a clear transfer plan — particularly in states with guaranteed transfer programs — are finding more deliberate support infrastructure than they would have five years ago.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Community college as a first step has historically been treated as a fallback rather than a strategy. The data increasingly supports treating it as a strategy — especially in states with guaranteed transfer programs that are now backed by real institutional infrastructure. The students who use the community college pathway strategically — who research their intended transfer institution's requirements before enrolling, who complete honors coursework, who build faculty relationships — consistently outperform their expectations.
Potential Benefits
  • Free tuition programs dramatically reduce cost for students who use the transfer pathway
  • 623 average enrollment gain demonstrates that access programs work when designed well
  • Statewide re-enrollment infrastructure creates support for non-traditional students who need guidance
Key Concerns
  • Transfer pathway requires more advance planning than direct admission — research and intentionality are non-optional
  • Credit transfer policies vary and can result in lost coursework if not researched carefully
  • Financial aid for transfer students is often less generous than for first-year students at four-year schools
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Community College Free Tuition Program Enrollment Gains' (Feb. 4, 2026)
  • University Business — '5 Higher Education Predictions for 2026' (Jan. 2, 2026)
  • Inside Higher Ed — Stop-Out Re-enrollment Initiative Coverage (Jan. 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • For students considering community college: research the specific guaranteed transfer requirements for your intended major — not just the institution
  • Verify credit transfer policies in your state before committing to a community college course sequence
  • Apply for financial aid at the community college level — many students don't realize they qualify
→ Action Steps
  1. Contact the transfer center (not just the general advising office) at any community college you're considering — they specialize in four-year pathway planning
  2. Research your state's guaranteed transfer program: California (UC/CSU), Texas (Top 10%), Florida, Virginia all have structured programs with different requirements
  3. Ask the four-year transfer target: what is the minimum GPA and course sequence required for admission to my intended major?
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'What is the guaranteed transfer arrangement between your community college and my intended four-year university?'
  • 'Which specific courses count toward my intended major at the transfer institution?'
  • 'What financial aid is available for transfer students specifically — separate from first-year aid pools?'

TOPIC 03State Funding / Florida

Florida's 12% Public University Tuition Hike Is Now Official — What Families Need to Recalculate

The Florida Board of Governors approved a 12% tuition increase at four-year institutions. Combined with the state's legislative climate around campus speech and curriculum, the Florida public university value proposition has materially changed.

The Florida Board of Governors voted 8-5 to approve a 12% tuition increase at four-year public universities and 9% at two-year colleges — effective for the upcoming academic year. The vote came as Florida universities also navigated ongoing legislative pressure on curriculum, DEI, and out-of-state enrollment.

The 12% increase is not isolated: it follows years of political intervention in Florida's public universities that has included administrative restructuring at New College (now functioning as a conservative model institution), restrictions on DEI programs, and curriculum oversight by politically appointed boards. The combination of higher cost and reduced institutional autonomy changes the Florida public university value proposition in ways that pure tuition data doesn't capture.

For families weighing Florida options: in-state Florida residents still receive exceptional value at UF and FSU even after the increase. Out-of-state families now face tuition levels that make private Florida institutions like the University of Miami and Rollins College genuinely competitive on net cost, and they warrant serious comparison.

🎓 Elite Institution Spotlight
Florida Public vs. Florida Private: The Recalculated Comparison
UF vs. University of Miami
UF in-state: still dramatically cheaper. UF out-of-state + 12% increase now approaching Miami's net cost for many families. Miami meets 100% of demonstrated need.
FSU vs. Rollins College
FSU in-state: still strong value. Out-of-state FSU now competes with Rollins on cost for middle-income families — and Rollins offers a more personal, smaller-school experience.
UCF vs. Florida Southern College
Large research university vs. small liberal arts. Net cost can favor private for merit-eligible students. UCF's scale advantage is in program breadth, not cost.
Florida Public System
Legislative pressure on DEI, curriculum, and governance is real and ongoing. Families should research each campus's specific environment, not just the system.
For Florida families: the right question is not 'public or private?' but 'which school — public or private — offers the best combination of program quality, net cost, campus culture, and institutional stability for my student?'
Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Florida's tuition increase confirms what I have been telling families for two years: the assumption that Florida public universities represent a straightforward value proposition needs to be updated. For in-state families, the value remains — but the gap between in-state public and strong Florida private institutions has narrowed. For out-of-state families, the public cost is now high enough that private net cost often competes. Run the net price calculators before making any assumption.
Potential Benefits
  • In-state Florida residents still receive meaningful value at UF and FSU even after the increase
  • Tuition revenue may help stabilize institutions facing budget pressure from other sources
  • Florida's public university system remains academically strong and well-regarded in most fields
Key Concerns
  • 12% increase accelerates the public-private cost convergence that is already underway nationally
  • Ongoing legislative intervention in curriculum and governance raises academic freedom concerns
  • Out-of-state families at Florida public universities now pay rates approaching many private school net costs
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Florida Board of Governors Approves 12% Tuition Increase' (Jan. 28, 2026)
  • Expert Admissions — 'Florida Senate Failed to Pass Out-of-State Enrollment Cap Proposal' (March 20, 2026)
  • CalMatters — Florida and California Higher Education Funding Comparison Coverage
✓ Action Checklist
  • Florida families: recalculate net cost at UF, FSU, and UCF with the 12% increase applied
  • Out-of-state families considering Florida: run net price calculators for University of Miami and Rollins before assuming state schools are cheaper
  • Ask Florida public universities: is the 12% increase reflected in your current net price calculator output?
→ Action Steps
  1. Use the UF, FSU, and UCF net price calculators directly — third-party databases may not yet reflect the 12% increase
  2. Compare Florida public out-of-state costs against University of Miami, Rollins, Stetson, and Florida Southern net cost
  3. For families with equity interests in Florida school districts: research how the out-of-state enrollment cap proposal (which failed) may be reintroduced in the future
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Does your net price calculator reflect the newly approved tuition increase?'
  • 'What is the projected tuition trajectory for the next four years based on current Board of Governors policy?'
  • 'For out-of-state applicants: what percentage of your student body is out-of-state, and is that changing?'

TOPIC 04International Funding / Foreign Gifts

Foreign Gifts Under Federal Scrutiny: What $5 Billion in International Funding Means for Research Universities

NPR reported that colleges have disclosed more than $5 billion in foreign gifts and contracts, with Harvard, Carnegie Mellon, MIT, and Stanford among the largest recipients. New State Department oversight is creating institutional caution.

NPR reported in the week of February 16, 2026 that U.S. colleges collectively report more than $5 billion annually in foreign gifts and contracts, with the largest recipients including Harvard, Carnegie Mellon, MIT, and Stanford. The State Department has taken over enforcement of Section 117 foreign gift reporting requirements, with a new federal reporting portal launched in January 2026 that subjects gift reporting to greater scrutiny.

The practical institutional response has been caution: several research universities have voluntarily curtailed relationships with certain foreign entities in anticipation of investigation risk, even where the relationships were previously considered unproblematic. This affects primarily graduate program funding, research partnerships, and faculty hiring from foreign institutions.

For families, the foreign gifts story has two angles: first, it is contributing to the same institutional caution that is reducing international graduate enrollment; second, it creates potential instability in research partnerships that may affect undergraduate research opportunities at institutions where foreign partnerships fund significant laboratory work.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The foreign gifts scrutiny is primarily a graduate and research enterprise issue — it does not directly affect the undergraduate admissions or financial aid process. But it contributes to the broader institutional risk environment that is causing research universities to make conservative decisions about hiring, partnerships, and program investment. For research-track undergraduates, the indirect effects on research opportunity availability are worth asking about.
Potential Benefits
  • Greater transparency in foreign funding relationships is a legitimate accountability goal
  • Most foreign partnerships involve straightforward academic exchange that benefits students
  • State Department oversight may provide clearer guidance than the prior ad hoc enforcement environment
Key Concerns
  • Institutional caution in response to investigation risk is reducing beneficial research partnerships
  • Chilling effect on international faculty hiring affects the diversity and quality of research teams
  • Graduate program funding instability ripples into undergraduate research opportunity availability
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Week in Review: Foreign Gifts Coverage' (Feb. 16, 2026)
  • NPR — Foreign Gifts and Contracts at U.S. Universities Reporting (Feb. 2026)
  • Deloitte Insights — 2026 Higher Education Trends: Foreign Funding Scrutiny Section
✓ Action Checklist
  • For research-track students: ask whether any research partnerships at your target school have been curtailed due to foreign funding scrutiny
  • Research whether your intended lab or research center has international partnerships that are currently under review
  • Ask about international faculty availability in your intended field — visa and scrutiny issues may affect who is teaching
→ Action Steps
  1. Search your target university's research news for any foreign partnership changes in the past 12 months
  2. For students interested in internationally collaborative research: ask specifically about current partnership status with institutions in China, Saudi Arabia, and other scrutiny-target countries
  3. Ask admissions how institutional caution around foreign relationships has affected graduate program funding in your intended field
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Have any research partnerships or funding relationships been curtailed due to foreign gift scrutiny?'
  • 'Are there any international research partnerships that affect programs or labs in my intended field?'
  • 'How has your institution responded to the new State Department foreign gift reporting requirements?'

TOPIC 05Endowment / Investment Returns

Strong Endowment Returns Look Good — But Don't Tell the Whole Story

Big university endowments averaged 8.2%–11.5% returns in FY2025. But with 8% endowment tax rates, mandatory spending drawdowns, and rising operating costs, the strong returns are already committed.

NACUBO-Commonfund's annual endowment study and TIFF Investment Management analysis found that big university endowments averaged 8.2% returns across the sector, with the largest endowments (over $1 billion) averaging 11.5% returns in FY2025. The strong performance numbers were widely reported as evidence of institutional financial health.

What the returns number alone doesn't capture: universities typically spend about 5% of investment earnings annually on operations. The new 8% endowment tax for top-tier schools consumes a meaningful additional portion of those earnings — plus the earnings themselves have to be realized through asset sales in an environment where universities prefer to hold long-term assets. Additionally, colleges also tapped their endowments at higher rates in FY2025, the NACUBO study found.

The practical result: strong endowment returns do not translate to financial flexibility if the returns are already committed to tax obligations, higher-than-historical spending drawdowns, and operating cost growth. Families should not read strong endowment returns as evidence of institutional financial ease.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Endowment returns are the higher education equivalent of a company's gross revenue — they look good but tell you little without the expense structure. The schools with the strongest financial positions in 2026 are those with large endowments, diversified revenue, manageable tax exposure, and demonstrated commitment to financial aid from specifically protected endowment funds. Ask your target school: what percentage of your endowment is 'restricted' to financial aid versus available for general operating use?
Potential Benefits
  • 8.2%–11.5% returns provide real financial cushion for institutions managing multiple cost pressures
  • Strong endowments support need-blind and full-need-met commitments that protect student aid
  • Diversified investment portfolios reduce exposure to any single asset class volatility
Key Concerns
  • 8% endowment tax at the top tier consumes a significant portion of otherwise available returns
  • Higher spending drawdown rates mean institutions are drawing down faster than ideal for long-term sustainability
  • Return volatility means strong FY2025 numbers don't guarantee FY2026 stability
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Big University Endowments Grew 11.5% in FY25, TIFF Says' (Nov. 2025)
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Colleges Also Tapped Their Endowments More in FY2025' (Feb. 12, 2026)
  • NACUBO-Commonfund Study of Endowments — FY2025 Results
✓ Action Checklist
  • Ask your target school what percentage of endowment funds are restricted to financial aid purposes
  • For schools in the 4% or 8% endowment tax tier: ask how the tax obligation affects their annual financial aid budget
  • Research whether your target school has publicly committed to a specific financial aid spending level regardless of endowment performance
→ Action Steps
  1. Find your target school's endowment spending policy in their annual financial report — most are publicly available
  2. Ask financial aid: 'Is your aid budget funded from restricted endowment funds that are specifically dedicated to aid, or from general operating funds?'
  3. Compare financial aid commitment language across schools — 'meet 100% of demonstrated need' is meaningfully different from 'offer a competitive aid package'
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'What percentage of your endowment is restricted to financial aid purposes?'
  • 'How is your endowment tax obligation affecting your financial aid budget for the coming year?'
  • 'Is your institutional aid commitment guaranteed for four years regardless of endowment performance?'

TOPIC 06AAC&U Annual Conference / Leadership

What College Presidents Said at AAC&U — And What It Means for Campus Culture

The AAC&U annual conference produced clear signals from college leadership about where institutions are betting on differentiation: economic mobility, experiential learning, and AI integration — with strong disagreement on how to resist federal pressure.

The American Association of Colleges and Universities annual meeting produced two notable outputs this week. Higher Ed Dive's coverage of top takeaways for college presidents emphasized the diversity of institutional responses to federal pressure: some presidents have chosen engagement with the administration, others have chosen resistance, and many are attempting careful navigation between both.

A Public Agenda analysis examining how colleges tailor student support services to improve economic mobility found that institutions with the most success are those that have made economic mobility an explicit institutional mission — tracking outcomes, building employer relationships, and providing targeted advising for low-income students throughout their enrollment.

The AAC&U's own employer survey, 'The Agility Imperative,' found that employers increasingly need students with diverse experiential opportunities — co-ops, internships, project-based learning, and civic engagement — alongside traditional academic credentials. The survey found institutions are not keeping pace with employer expectations for experiential depth.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The AAC&U conference story matters to families because it maps where institutional energy is being directed. Colleges investing in economic mobility infrastructure, experiential learning, and transparent outcome tracking are the ones most likely to produce strong employment outcomes for their graduates. The colleges that are primarily managing political compliance are the ones most likely to be distracted from the educational mission. Ask admissions what their president's top institutional priorities are for the next three years — the answer tells you what will get institutional resources.
Potential Benefits
  • Growing focus on economic mobility creates more targeted support for students from lower-income backgrounds
  • Experiential learning expansion — co-ops, internships, project-based programs — improves employment outcomes
  • AAC&U framework for outcome accountability creates shared standards for measuring institutional effectiveness
Key Concerns
  • Political compliance demands are consuming institutional leadership attention that might otherwise go to educational quality
  • Division among college presidents on how to respond to federal pressure creates inconsistent institutional cultures
  • Employer expectations for experiential depth are growing faster than institutions' ability to respond
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Top Takeaways for College Presidents from AAC&U's Conference' (Feb. 3, 2026)
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'How 3 College Leaders Work to Boost Economic Mobility' (Jan. 30, 2026)
  • AAC&U — 'The Agility Imperative: 2025 Employer Survey' (2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Ask admissions what the president's stated top institutional priorities are for the coming year
  • Research whether your target school has explicit economic mobility outcome tracking and reporting
  • Ask about experiential learning requirements — which schools have co-op, internship, or capstone project requirements built into the degree?
→ Action Steps
  1. Find and read your target school's president's most recent 'State of the University' address — it reveals institutional priorities more honestly than marketing materials
  2. Ask admissions to describe the most recent employer partnership or experiential learning initiative the school has launched
  3. Research your target school's employment outcome data by major — some schools publish this; others you need to ask for
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'What are your president's stated top three institutional priorities for the next academic year?'
  • 'Does your institution track and publish employment outcomes by major?'
  • 'What experiential learning opportunities — internships, co-ops, project-based learning — are built into my intended program?'

Guidance Counselor Corner

What Every Counselor Should Know and Share This Week

This week counselors have three critical conversations to prepare for. First: the private loan qualification gap affects a large percentage of middle-income families who are building college plans around borrowing capacity they haven't verified. Encourage every family to pre-qualify during junior year — the conversation costs nothing and prevents April disasters.

The Florida 12% tuition increase requires an immediate update to any Florida family's financial planning. For in-state students, the value remains strong. For out-of-state students, the calculation now requires a direct comparison to strong Florida private institutions. Do not allow families to assume that public equals affordable in Florida without running current numbers.

The endowment returns story requires translation for families who may read strong returns as financial health. Help families understand the difference between endowment returns, spending policy, tax obligations, and specifically restricted financial aid funds. The question 'is your aid budget funded from restricted endowment funds?' is one every counselor should teach families to ask.
