TOPIC 01Research Funding / Courts

Courts Permanently Block NIH Indirect Cost Cap — But the Fight Continues at Other Agencies

A federal appeals court upheld the permanent block on NIH's 15% indirect cost reimbursement cap. But DOE, NSF, and DOD have adopted similar caps — and those cases are still pending.

A federal appeals court upheld the permanent block on the National Institutes of Health's attempted cap of research overhead reimbursement at 15%, following a January 5 lower court ruling that made the block permanent. More than 20 state attorneys general had sued, along with hospital associations and higher education groups, arguing the cap violated Congressional appropriations authority and federal administrative law.

The ruling is significant: courts ruled that the Trump administration cannot cap indirect cost reimbursements through executive action when Congress has not authorized the change. Research universities typically negotiate overhead rates above 50% — rates that cover building maintenance, equipment, safety compliance, and administrative support for federally funded research. The cap would have been devastating for institutions like Johns Hopkins, where research is a core financial and programmatic pillar.

But the administration responded immediately: the Departments of Energy, Defense, and NSF all adopted similar caps shortly after the NIH ruling, triggering new rounds of litigation. An appeals court upheld the block on NIH's cap. The DOE and DOD appeals are still pending. NSF withdrew its appeal without explanation. The practical status: NIH overhead is protected; DOE and DOD overhead remains in legal limbo.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The court victories on indirect costs are genuinely good news for research universities and the researchers who depend on them. But they are victories in a long war, not the war's end. The administration's approach — implement the cap, get blocked, re-implement at a different agency, get blocked again — is designed to wear down the institutional capacity to fight and to create ongoing uncertainty that deters research investment. Families considering research universities should ask about each school's funding stability in this ongoing environment.
Potential Benefits

  • Congressional FY2026 budget preserved research funding largely intact despite White House proposals
  • Legal victories establish precedent that executive action cannot override Congressional appropriations
Key Concerns
  • Administration's agency-by-agency approach creates ongoing litigation burden for universities
  • Uncertainty persists even where courts have ruled — institutions remain reluctant to commit to long-term research investments
  • Research programs that paused during freeze periods lost irreplaceable time and data

TOPIC 02Harvard / Federal Litigation

Harvard vs. The Administration: What the Ongoing Battle Means for Applicants

Harvard refused to comply with administration demands, lost $2.2B in research funding, won preliminary court rulings, and now faces a DOJ admissions lawsuit. The fight is defining elite higher education's future.

The Trump administration froze $2.2 billion in Harvard's research funding in April 2025 after Harvard declined to adopt sweeping demands including changes to admissions, disciplinary processes, international student enrollment, and campus governance. Harvard sued in late April, and in September, U.S. District Judge Allison Burroughs ruled against the funding freeze, finding First Amendment violations and arbitrary conduct.

In the week of January 19–23, the legal conflict expanded. The Department of Defense announced it would cut academic ties with Harvard, affecting military research partnerships. And the administration filed a DOJ lawsuit alleging Harvard was withholding admissions data — applicant-level essays and test scores disaggregated by race — from an investigation into post-SFFA compliance.

Harvard's response remained consistent: it would not comply with demands it characterized as unconstitutional, while continuing to acknowledge legitimate areas for improvement in campus climate and academic governance. The administration's response has been to escalate through every available mechanism: funding freezes, patent threats, SEVP revocation (blocked by courts), admissions investigations, and now a direct DOJ lawsuit.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
For families with Harvard on their list, I want to be clear: Harvard is still one of the finest universities in the world, and it is actively defending the institutional autonomy that makes it that. The ongoing litigation means financial uncertainty in research programs, but the undergraduate experience — course offerings, faculty quality, financial aid — is being actively protected by institutional commitment and endowment resources. The question families should ask is not whether Harvard is worth applying to, but whether the campus environment during this period of sustained external pressure is the right fit for their student.
Potential Benefits
  • Harvard's litigation is winning important early court victories that benefit the entire sector
  • Harvard's large endowment ($53B) provides financial resilience that most institutions lack
  • Research programs are partially restored by court order and continuing
Key Concerns
  • Ongoing uncertainty creates planning difficulty for students and faculty at all affected institutions
  • DOJ admissions lawsuit adds a new legal front and potential admissions process disruption
  • DOD partnership cuts reduce research and career pathway options for students interested in defense-related work

TOPIC 03Federal Budget / Student Aid

Congress Passes FY2026 Budget: What Was Preserved and What Wasn't

Congress rejected the White House's most aggressive higher education cuts — preserving NIH, NSF, Pell Grants, and core student aid programs. Understanding what survived tells you a great deal about what is politically durable.

The FY2026 appropriations bill, passed by Congress in late January 2026, largely preserved federal funding for higher education and research at current levels — a significant departure from the White House's budget requests, which had proposed slashing NSF by more than 50% and NIH by nearly 40%. The final bill added approximately $217 million above the prior year for education programs.

The preservation of Pell Grants and core student aid programs reflected bipartisan consensus that base access funding cannot be cut without creating politically unacceptable consequences for students in every congressional district. The American Association of University Professors described Congress as 'defending the power of the purse' against executive overreach.

What was not preserved: the administration's transfers of programs via interagency agreements — TRIO to Labor, school safety grants to HHS — continued regardless of appropriations, creating a dual-track of Congressional funding and executive program restructuring. The practical consequence is that some programs are funded but administered by agencies with different priorities.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The FY2026 budget outcome is meaningfully better than it might have been. But 'flat budgets' in an environment of rising costs are effectively cuts in real purchasing power. The Times Higher Education reporting described this as a 'double-edged sword' — Congress preserved the headline numbers but the administration retains enough discretion over spending priorities that the actual research enterprise may still be squeezed. Pell Grants are secure. Research funding is better than feared. But program-level instability continues.
Potential Benefits
  • Pell Grants preserved and modestly increased — the most direct support for lower-income students is stable
  • NIH and NSF budgets preserved roughly at current levels despite White House proposals
  • TRIO and GEAR UP programs retained Congressional funding despite administration transfer attempts
Key Concerns
  • Flat budgets = real purchasing power decline given 4.4% cost growth forecast for the sector (Moody's)
  • Administration's interagency agreement transfers continue regardless of appropriations
  • FY2027 budget process will bring another round of proposed cuts — this is an annual battle
Key Articles This Week

  • Times Higher Education — 'US Science Budgets Have Been Spared. So Why Is No One Celebrating?' (March 2026)
  • Inside Higher Ed — Pell Grant and TRIO funding coverage (Jan./Feb. 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Confirm that your target school's federal student aid eligibility is intact — look up any accreditation or compliance issues
  • If you are a Pell Grant recipient or likely to be: confirm your eligibility calculation before selecting a school
  • Track FY2027 budget proposals when they emerge in spring 2026 — they will tell you where the next round of cuts is targeted
→ Action Steps

  2. Ask your target school's financial aid office: are your institutional aid commitments protected from federal budget changes?
  3. Monitor ACE and NASFAA policy newsletters — they publish the clearest summaries of federal budget impacts on aid programs
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Is your institution's federal aid eligibility current and unrestricted?'
  • 'How does your financial aid package treat Pell Grant eligibility — is it additive to institutional aid or substituted?'
  • 'Are TRIO or GEAR UP services available at your institution, and are they fully funded for the coming year?'

TOPIC 04DEI Policy / Legal

Fourth Circuit Lets Anti-DEI Executive Orders Stand — What Colleges Must Now Do

The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the injunction against the administration's major anti-DEI executive orders. Institutions must now review scholarships, hiring, and programming for compliance.

The Fourth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals vacated a preliminary injunction that had blocked two major anti-DEI executive orders from affecting colleges and universities. The ruling came on February 10, 2026, following an earlier pause by the same panel. The practical effect: the executive orders' requirements now apply to federal contractors and grant recipients — which includes virtually every major university.

Inside Higher Ed reported that Indiana University is reviewing scholarships under the new threat. The Education Department has investigated Tufts University over independent voter research, treating nonpartisan civic participation research as potentially suspect. The Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE) documented that one in seven faculty reported being disciplined or threatened due to teaching, research, or speech during 2024–2025.

For families, the practical question is how each target school is navigating compliance: which scholarships, programs, and initiatives are being reviewed or restructured, and how is the institution communicating about these changes to prospective students?

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The DEI executive orders are creating real changes to campus programs that affect students — particularly first-generation students, students from underrepresented communities, and students who benefited from targeted support programs. The institutions that are managing this most effectively are those with transparent governance: they communicate clearly about which programs are changing and why, and they have alternative support mechanisms in place for students who relied on DEI-adjacent programs.
Potential Benefits
  • Some institutions are developing DEI-alternative programs that serve similar populations under different legal frameworks
  • The ruling's reach is still being interpreted — some programs may be protected under existing law
  • Congressional research funding preservation reduces the leverage the administration has over some programs
Key Concerns
  • One in seven faculty reported being disciplined or threatened due to speech in 2024–25 — a chilling effect on academic freedom
  • Targeted scholarships and support programs for first-gen and underrepresented students are under review across institutions
  • Compliance reviews create administrative burden that diverts resources from education
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Week in Review: Trump Scores Legal Victory on Anti-DEI Orders' (Feb. 16, 2026)
  • AAC&U — Media Coverage of DEI Program Changes at Texas Institutions (Feb. 2026)
  • FIRE — Faculty Survey on Discipline and Speech Threats, 2024–25 Academic Year
✓ Action Checklist
  • Ask target schools directly: which scholarships and support programs are currently under review due to executive orders?
  • Research whether any support programs you plan to use (first-gen services, cultural centers, targeted scholarships) have been restructured
  • Understand the difference between programs that were directly funded by DEI-labeled grants and programs that have independent institutional funding
→ Action Steps
  1. Search your target school's news page for 'DEI' or 'executive order' to identify which programs have been affected
  2. Contact student services directly — not admissions — to ask about the current status of support programs
  3. For scholarship applicants: verify that any scholarship you are considering remains available and legally structured
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Are any scholarship programs I might be eligible for currently under legal review?'
  • 'How has your institution responded to the anti-DEI executive orders in terms of program structure?'
  • 'What alternative support mechanisms exist for first-generation and underrepresented students given recent policy changes?'

TOPIC 05Accreditation / AIM Committee

The AIM Accreditation Reform Committee: What It Means That New Accreditors Are Coming

The January 2026 launch of the Accreditation, Innovation, and Modernization committee signals a deliberate effort to restructure who controls the gateway to federal student aid. Families need to understand what accreditation actually means.

The Education Department formally launched the Accreditation, Innovation, and Modernization (AIM) committee in January 2026, with a mandate to simplify accreditor recognition, combat 'cost and credential inflation,' ban DEI discrimination standards, and focus accreditation on measurable outcomes. The committee will hold sessions in April and May 2026.

Parallel to the AIM process, new accreditors are positioning to enter the market. A consortium of conservative state university systems in Florida, Texas, Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, and Tennessee is developing a new accrediting body. Another organization, the National Association for Academic Excellence, has drawn comparisons to the previously discredited ACICS — the for-profit accreditor whose collapse contributed to the collapse of multiple for-profit universities.

The stakes are real: accreditation determines whether a college is eligible for federal financial aid — Pell Grants, federal loans, and federal work-study. A degree from an unaccredited institution is not recognized by most employers or graduate schools. Families considering any non-traditional program should verify accreditation status before committing.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Accreditation reform is the sleeper issue in higher education policy right now. The existing regional accreditors — the seven major bodies that cover virtually every institution families consider — are not at immediate risk. But the creation of new accreditors with different standards creates risk for students who enroll in newly-accredited programs whose quality is untested. The short-term rule: stick to institutions accredited by one of the seven major regional accreditors unless you have specific information that a newer accreditor maintains equivalent standards.
Potential Benefits
  • Reform of accreditation could reduce barriers to quality new programs and institutions
  • Outcome-focused standards could create more accountability for poor-performing programs
  • Streamlined recognition process could accelerate innovation in non-degree and short-term credentials
Key Concerns
  • New accreditors with lower standards could expose students to unrecognized degrees and wasted tuition
  • DEI ban in accreditation standards may reduce attention to equity in educational access and outcomes
  • Conservative state consortium accreditor raises questions about political influence over academic quality standards
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Jan. 27 AIM Committee Federal Register Notice Coverage' (Jan. 27, 2026)
  • Changing Higher Ed Podcast — Accreditation Reform Analysis (Dec. 2025)
  • AcademicJobs.com — Dept. of Education Dismantling Status 2026 Analysis
✓ Action Checklist
  • Verify that every institution on your list is accredited by one of the seven major regional accreditors
  • For any online or alternative program: verify accreditor recognition and employer acceptance before enrolling
  • Track AIM committee outcomes in April–May 2026 — the resulting guidance will affect which programs are eligible for aid
→ Action Steps
  1. Look up accreditation status at ope.ed.gov — every federally funded institution is listed with its accreditor
  2. For online or non-traditional programs: ask employers in your intended field whether they recognize degrees from the accreditor in question
  3. Monitor NASFAA and ACE policy updates for AIM committee outcomes and new accreditor recognitions
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Is your institution accredited by one of the seven major regional accreditors?'
  • 'Has your accreditation been subject to any review, warning, or probationary status in the past five years?'
  • 'If a new accreditor is involved in any programs I am considering, is that accreditor recognized by employers in my intended field?'

TOPIC 06College Closures / Mergers

Small College Closures Accelerate: What the Merger and Closure Trend Means for Families

Labouré College of Healthcare closes this summer; its nursing programs absorbed by Curry College. Multiple Catholic institutions merged or at risk. Fitch, Moody's, and S&P all predict a difficult financial year for small colleges.

Higher Ed Dive reported in early February 2026 that Labouré College of Healthcare, a Massachusetts institution, will cease operations this summer with its nursing programs absorbed by nearby Curry College. Multiple small Catholic institutions are in financial distress, with dwindling enrollment and rising costs. A California institution also announced closure in February.

The financial ratings agencies are unanimous in their 2026 outlook: Fitch Ratings issued a 'deteriorating' outlook; Moody's estimated that costs will grow 4.4% while revenue grows only 3.5%; S&P projected continued financial strain. The Deloitte 2026 Higher Education Trends report identified institutions under 1,000 students as most vulnerable.

For families, this is primarily a warning about where not to apply — or where to apply with a clear contingency plan. Institutions under enrollment pressure, with declining retention, or with recent leadership turmoil are at elevated closure risk. The risk is real: if an institution closes after a student enrolls, the student faces disruption, potential credit loss, and a break in financial aid continuity.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
I advise families to treat institutional financial stability as a selection criterion, not just a background concern. For students considering small colleges — which may offer genuinely excellent education in the right circumstances — the question is whether the institution has the financial stability to deliver on its promises over four years. The warning signs are observable: declining enrollment, leadership turnover, public accreditation warnings, and Moody's or Fitch rating downgrades. Look before you commit.
Potential Benefits
  • Mergers and absorptions often protect students' academic progress and credit continuity
  • Some small institutions are genuinely stabilizing through innovative programming and enrollment recovery
  • Community colleges and state universities provide stable alternatives for students seeking small-school environments
Key Concerns
  • Fitch 'deteriorating' outlook for the sector as a whole means closure risk is elevated broadly
  • Institutions under 1,000 students face the highest vulnerability — Deloitte identifies these as most at risk
  • Closure after enrollment can disrupt financial aid, credit transfer, and career trajectory
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — College Closures and Mergers Coverage (Feb. 2026)
  • Deloitte Insights — 2026 Higher Education Trends Report
  • Fitch Ratings — 2026 Higher Education Sector Outlook (Jan. 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Research the financial stability of every college on your list, especially smaller institutions
  • Check for recent accreditation warnings or probationary status at any institution you are considering
  • Ask smaller colleges about their enrollment trend over the past three years — declining enrollment is the primary closure predictor
→ Action Steps
  1. Search for each target institution's name + 'Moody's' or 'Fitch' or 'financial warning' to check for public ratings actions
  2. Look up each school's Common Data Set enrollment trends over the past 5 years — consistent declines are a warning sign
  3. For Catholic and small private colleges: check diocesan and board news for any governance or financial restructuring announcements
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Has your institution received any accreditation warnings, probationary status, or credit rating downgrades in the past three years?'
  • 'What is your enrollment trend over the past five years?'
  • 'Does your institution have a formal contingency plan for students if financial difficulties arise?'

Guidance Counselor Corner

What Every Counselor Should Know and Share This Week

This week counselors have an important legal-landscape update to share with families. The court victories on NIH indirect costs are meaningful but the administration is routing around them through other agencies. The message for families: federal research funding is in a better place than it might have been, but institutional stability remains variable and requires due diligence.

The DEI executive order ruling is the story counselors most need to translate for families with students who relied on targeted support programs. The programs themselves may still exist under different names or frameworks — or they may not. Ask each institution directly, not generically. The answer varies by institution and by program.

The closure trend demands attention for counselors advising students considering smaller institutions. The financial stability indicators — enrollment trends, accreditation status, leadership stability, endowment size — should be part of every college research conversation, not just an afterthought.
