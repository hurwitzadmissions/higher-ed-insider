TOPIC 01Endowment Tax / Elite Finances

The Endowment Tax Is Real and the Numbers Are Enormous

Harvard faces a $368M annual tax bill. Princeton is cutting 5–10% of department budgets. Stanford laid off 363 employees. The One Big Beautiful Bill's tiered endowment tax is reshaping elite university finances right now.

The American Enterprise Institute estimates that approximately 20 universities will be subject to the new endowment tax in its first year. Harvard, with its $53 billion endowment, faces the highest bill at approximately $368 million annually — compared to $44 million under the prior 1.4% rate. Yale faces approximately $280 million; Princeton more than $217 million. MIT and Stanford face comparable nine-figure bills.

The responses have been immediate and concrete. Stanford announced 363 layoffs. Princeton's president asked all departments and units to prepare plans for 5% and 10% permanent budget cuts to be phased in over the next three years. Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences reduced its budget. The actions come simultaneously with federal research funding freezes — a double financial squeeze that is producing real structural changes.

The new tiered system charges 1.4% for endowments of $500,000–$749,999 per student, 4% for $750,000–$1.99M per student, and 8% for $2M or more per student. The 4% tier includes Notre Dame, Dartmouth, Rice, Penn, Washington University in St. Louis, and Vanderbilt. Duke and Emory are near the 4% threshold.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
These are not numbers that affect institutional rankings in the short term, but they affect everything that shapes the quality of the educational experience — research funding, staff-to-student ratios, program breadth, financial aid generosity. The institutions most impacted are precisely the ones families have spent a generation targeting as the gold standard. My advice: ask admissions directly how the endowment tax is affecting their budget, and specifically whether financial aid commitments are secure.
Potential Benefits
  • Endowment tax revenue flows to the general Treasury — some argue it represents appropriate accountability for tax-exempt wealth
  • Institutions with diversified revenue beyond endowment are better insulated
  • Congressional action preserved Pell Grants and student aid in FY2026 budget
Key Concerns
  • $368M annual tax for Harvard represents a 7-fold increase over prior obligation — not easily absorbed
  • Stanford layoffs and Princeton budget cuts signal real program reductions ahead
  • Schools at the 4% tier (Notre Dame, Dartmouth, Vanderbilt) face less-publicized but still significant impacts

TOPIC 02AI in Education / Faculty

Nine in Ten Faculty Say AI Will Hurt Critical Thinking. That Is a Planning Signal.

A landmark AAC&U survey found 90% of faculty believe generative AI will diminish student critical thinking, and 95% predict increased student over-reliance. These are not fringe concerns — they are the mainstream faculty view.

The American Association of Colleges and Universities survey, published January 21, 2026, found that nine in ten faculty members believe generative AI will diminish students' critical thinking skills. Ninety-five percent predict it will increase students' over-reliance on AI tools. These findings are not from AI skeptics at fringe institutions — they represent the mainstream view across American higher education faculty.

AAC&U President Lynn Pasquerella noted that many faculty feel their colleges provide too little support around AI policy and implementation. The Chronicle of Higher Education framed the findings starkly: 'Faculty Are Overwhelmed and Conflicted by AI.' The survey found that while faculty recognize AI's inevitability, most believe its adoption is outpacing institutions' ability to integrate it thoughtfully.

For families, this creates a concrete research task: how is each target school structuring its AI policy, and what is the faculty governance mechanism for AI decisions? Schools with clear, faculty-governed AI frameworks are more likely to protect the intellectual development that makes a degree from that institution valuable.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
I take the faculty AI anxiety seriously because it reflects a real tension. The skills that make a college degree valuable — critical analysis, original argument, synthesis of complex information — are precisely the skills that AI can simulate without developing. If students use AI to produce work without developing the underlying capabilities, they will graduate with credentials that don't reflect genuine competence. The institutions that will protect the value of their degrees are the ones with serious, faculty-governed AI frameworks — not the ones that simply ban AI or simply allow everything.
Potential Benefits
  • AI integration can improve access to tutoring, feedback, and personalized learning support
  • Early institutional investment in AI literacy prepares students for a workforce where AI competence is required
  • Some institutions are developing genuinely thoughtful AI integration policies that preserve academic rigor
Key Concerns
  • 90% of faculty believe AI will hurt critical thinking — the people closest to the learning process are deeply concerned
  • Institutional AI policies are often still being developed, leaving faculty and students without clear guidance
  • AI-generated work that passes academic review undermines the credential's signal to employers
Key Articles This Week
  • Chronicle of Higher Education — 'Faculty Are Overwhelmed and Conflicted by AI' (Jan. 21, 2026)
  • Inside Higher Ed — 'Survey: Faculty Say AI Is Impactful — But Not in a Good Way' (Jan. 21, 2026)
  • AAC&U — 'Faculty Express Deep Concern Over AI's Impact on Higher Education' (Jan. 21, 2026)
✓ Action Checklist
  • Ask each target school about their formal AI policy — is it faculty-governed or administration-imposed?
  • Research whether your intended major department has specific AI use guidelines
  • Ask during campus visits: how does your school ensure that AI doesn't undermine the development of genuine competence?
→ Action Steps
  1. Search each target school's faculty senate or academic policies page for 'AI policy' or 'generative AI'
  2. Look for courses at your target school that explicitly address AI literacy and critical thinking — their presence signals institutional seriousness
  3. Ask faculty or current students (not admissions staff) how AI policy is actually enforced
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Does your institution have a faculty-governed policy on AI use in coursework?'
  • 'How does your department define appropriate vs. inappropriate AI use in assignments?'
  • 'What support does your institution provide to help students develop skills that AI cannot replace?'

TOPIC 03Global Rankings / Research Competitiveness

Chinese Universities Are Surging in Global Rankings as American Institutions Slip

The New York Times reported January 15, 2026 that Chinese universities are rising in global rankings as U.S. schools slip — a direct consequence of federal research funding disruptions that has long-term implications.

The New York Times reported on January 15, 2026 that Chinese universities are surging in global research rankings as American institutions slip, a trend multiple analysts attributed directly to the disruption of U.S. federal research funding. The pattern was anticipated: when researchers are cut off from funding, their publication output falls; when institutions abroad recruit displaced U.S. researchers, their output rises.

The timing matters. Global research rankings typically lag institutional changes by 2–5 years. The ranking shifts visible in January 2026 reflect 2024–2025 research output — before the worst of the funding disruptions. The impact of 2025's grant terminations and researcher departures will not fully appear in rankings for another 2–4 years.

For families choosing research universities, this is a long-horizon consideration. The institutions best positioned to maintain their research leadership are those with the most diversified funding sources: large endowments, strong industry partnerships, and state funding support. Pure federal grant dependence has become a vulnerability.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
I want to be precise about what this means for families. The top American universities are not becoming mediocre. What is changing is the rate of research advancement, the ability to retain the most ambitious graduate researchers, and the pipeline of discoveries that flows from cutting-edge work. For families whose students are planning careers in research-intensive fields — medicine, biosciences, climate, AI — the research university environment they enter will affect their career trajectories. Choose institutions with diversified research funding.
Potential Benefits
  • Competition from international institutions creates innovation incentives for American universities
  • Some research universities are accelerating industry and philanthropic partnerships to replace federal funding
  • Courts have preserved meaningful protections for research funding in ongoing litigation
Key Concerns
  • Rankings slippage reflects real research output decline, not just perception
  • Brain drain to institutions abroad permanently shifts research capacity — talent is hard to recover once relocated
  • 2–4 year lag means the full impact of 2025 disruptions is not yet visible in current rankings
Key Articles This Week
  • The New York Times — 'Chinese Universities Surge in Global Rankings as US Schools Slip' (Jan. 15, 2026)
  • Higher Ed Dive — 'Will Higher Education Researchers Leave for Opportunities Abroad?' (Jan. 5, 2026)
  • Deloitte Insights — 2026 Higher Education Trends Report
✓ Action Checklist
  • For research-track students: ask specifically about faculty retention in your intended field
  • Research your target school's funding diversification: what percentage of research funding comes from industry vs. federal?
  • Check recent global ranking movements for your target schools in your intended field
→ Action Steps
  1. Use QS World University Rankings or Times Higher Education subject rankings to research your intended field specifically — not just overall university rankings
  2. Ask each research university: what is your three-year research funding strategy given current federal uncertainty?
  3. For STEM applicants: ask about industry partnership labs and research centers that operate independent of federal grants
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'What percentage of your institution's research funding comes from non-federal sources?'
  • 'How is your institution responding to the potential loss of researchers to international institutions?'
  • 'Have your global research rankings in my intended field changed in the past two years?'

TOPIC 04Financial Aid / FAFSA

The New FAFSA Earnings Warning: What It Means That 23% of Schools Trigger the Flag

A new FAFSA earnings indicator warns students when they show interest in institutions whose graduates earn less than high school diploma holders. Twenty-three percent of institutions trigger the warning. Families need to understand what it does — and doesn't — measure.

Beginning with the current FAFSA cycle, the Department of Education added an earnings indicator that warns students when they show interest in an institution where graduates' median earnings fall below those of high school diploma holders. The warning appears during the application process, before a student submits.

Twenty-three percent of institutions currently trigger this warning. The policy has two intended audiences: students who might be attending low-value institutions without realizing it, and institutions that may need to improve outcomes to avoid the reputational damage of the flag. Advocates worry it will divert students from perfectly good programs in fields with historically lower starting salaries — education, social work, the arts — toward purely income-maximizing choices.

For families evaluating college choices, the indicator is a starting point, not a conclusion. Median earnings data does not capture earnings 10 years out, does not account for graduate school enrollment rates, and does not reflect the non-economic value of education. What it does effectively flag is genuinely low-ROI programs and institutions where degree completion rates are low.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The earnings indicator is a blunt instrument but it is asking the right question: does this degree improve your economic position? The problem is that 'median earnings two years after graduation' is a poor proxy for that question. Social workers, teachers, and artists earn less early and often earn their greatest returns through career advancement over decades. I advise families to use the indicator as a red-flag screen — if a school is flagged, ask why — but not as a positive selection criterion.
Potential Benefits
  • Creates public accountability for genuinely low-value programs and institutions
  • Helps students identify institutions with poor completion rates before committing
  • Encourages institutions to improve outcomes and track graduate employment more carefully
Key Concerns
  • Median early earnings are a poor proxy for lifetime educational value
  • Arts, education, and social work programs will be systematically penalized regardless of their genuine quality
  • 23% of institutions flagged may cause students to avoid legitimate programs in undervalued fields
Key Articles This Week
  • Changing Higher Ed Podcast — 2026 Higher Education Planning and Outlook (Dec. 2025)
  • FAFSA Online Portal — New Earnings Indicator Implementation Documentation
  • Lumina Foundation — Short-term Credential Program Quality Assessment Research
✓ Action Checklist
  • Check whether any institution on your list triggers the FAFSA earnings indicator — and if so, research why
  • Look beyond early earnings data: check 10-year earnings data available through the College Scorecard
  • For fields with lower starting salaries: ask schools specifically about career trajectory and graduate school enrollment rates
→ Action Steps
  1. Use the College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov) to look up 6-year and 10-year earnings data for your intended major at each target school
  2. If a school triggers the earnings warning, read their specific program outcome data — institution-level data can mask strong program-level outcomes
  3. Ask admissions: what is the graduate employment rate in my intended field within one year of graduation?
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Does your institution or my intended program trigger the FAFSA earnings indicator?'
  • 'What are the median earnings of your graduates in my intended field at 1, 5, and 10 years?'
  • 'What percentage of graduates in my intended program go on to graduate school, and how does that affect your earnings data?'

TOPIC 05Student Loans / One Big Beautiful Bill

The One Big Beautiful Bill's Student Loan Provisions: What Takes Effect in July 2026

The administration's signature legislation eliminates Grad PLUS loans, caps professional program borrowing, and restructures income-driven repayment. If your student is considering graduate or professional school, the financial math just changed.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in summer 2025, includes significant changes to graduate student financing that take effect in July 2026. The most impactful: elimination of Grad PLUS loans (which allowed graduate students to borrow up to the full cost of attendance), a new cap on professional program borrowing, and less generous income-driven repayment terms for graduate borrowers.

The impact is most concentrated in law, medicine, MBA, and other professional programs where tuition commonly exceeds $60,000–$80,000 per year. For these programs, Grad PLUS was the primary financing mechanism for students without family wealth. Its elimination will either price out lower-income prospective graduate students, increase institutional aid costs, or both.

For families with students currently applying to or planning for graduate school: the financial planning conversation needs to happen now. The July 2026 implementation is real, and the borrowing capacity assumptions that have governed graduate school financial planning for the past decade no longer apply.

🎓 Elite Institution Spotlight
Grad PLUS Elimination: Impact by Elite Professional Program
Medical Schools
Typical medical school costs $60K–$80K/year. Grad PLUS covered everything above federal loan limits. Its elimination will most impact low-income and first-gen MD aspirants at private medical schools.
Law Schools
Top law schools average $65K–$70K/year in tuition alone. Harvard Law, Yale Law, and Columbia Law have large endowments to offer institutional aid — but the demand will spike dramatically.
MBA Programs
Full-time MBA at top schools runs $80K–$100K/year all-in. Business school endowments vary widely. Programs at universities with smaller endowments have less institutional aid capacity.
Public Health, Social Work, Education
Lower-earning fields with high debt loads. Grad PLUS was often the only viable financing mechanism. Loss is disproportionate for students in these fields.
If your student aspires to professional school, the undergraduate years are now also the years to build a financial plan for graduate school. The math has changed fundamentally.
Dr. Hurwitz's Take
The Grad PLUS elimination is, in my view, the single most significant change in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act for families in the college planning stage. Medical school, law school, and MBA programs have been accessible to qualified students from non-wealthy families precisely because Grad PLUS allowed unlimited federal borrowing. That access is being structurally reduced. Families with graduate school aspirations need to model the new financial math now — before those students select undergraduate institutions whose graduate programs they intend to continue.
Potential Benefits
  • Caps on professional borrowing may reduce some institutions' incentive to raise graduate tuition without accountability
  • New accountability structures require low-ROI programs to improve outcomes or lose aid eligibility
  • Pell Grants were preserved and basic federal aid for undergraduates was not reduced
Key Concerns
  • Grad PLUS elimination reduces graduate school access for students from non-wealthy families
  • Medical, law, and MBA programs will see the sharpest access impact
  • Less generous income-driven repayment means higher monthly payments for graduate borrowers
Key Articles This Week
  • Higher Ed Dive — '6 Higher Education Trends to Watch in 2026' (Jan. 8, 2026)
  • American Council on Education — One Big Beautiful Bill Act Higher Education Provisions Analysis
  • ACE dotEDU Podcast — Graduate Loan Caps and Professional Degree Definitions
✓ Action Checklist
  • If graduate or professional school is in your student's plans, model the new borrowing limits now
  • Ask every graduate/professional program your student is considering: what institutional aid is available post-Grad PLUS?
  • Research whether your intended professional program has responded to the Grad PLUS elimination with increased institutional aid
→ Action Steps
  1. Access the new federal loan limit tables (studentaid.gov) to understand the specific caps for your intended program
  2. Ask each graduate program: how are you responding to the July 2026 Grad PLUS elimination?
  3. Research whether your intended professional school has an endowment large enough to offer meaningful institutional aid to replace lost federal borrowing capacity
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'How has your institution responded to the elimination of Grad PLUS loans starting July 2026?'
  • 'What institutional aid will be available for graduate students who can no longer borrow up to the cost of attendance?'
  • 'How will the new income-driven repayment terms affect your program's graduate debt burden?'

TOPIC 06State Policy / Admissions

California's Automatic Admission Law: What SB 640 Does and Doesn't Do

Governor Newsom signed SB 640, granting automatic CSU admission to qualified California high school students. It solves one problem — access — while creating others around preparation and fit.

California Senate Bill 640, effective January 2026, grants automatic admission to the California State University system for qualified California high school students. The law eliminates the application process for eligible students — instead, CSU uses data from the CaliforniaColleges.edu platform to identify and notify eligible students directly.

The bill addresses two problems simultaneously: tens of thousands of qualified students who never apply because the application process is unfamiliar or intimidating, and CSU campuses experiencing enrollment declines that threaten their financial stability. Its author, Senator Christopher Cabaldon, described going to college as currently no different in difficulty from going to middle school to high school — and argued there is no reason it should be.

The practical planning question for families: automatic admission creates access but not guarantee of preparation or fit. Students admitted automatically to CSU campuses they did not actively research may find themselves enrolled at schools that are not well-matched to their goals. The law is good for access — it requires more work from students and families on research and intentionality.

Dr. Hurwitz's Take
Automatic admission laws are a genuine access tool and I support their intent. But access and fit are different things. A student automatically admitted to California State University Dominguez Hills who wanted to be at Cal Poly San Luis Obispo has access but not the right placement. Families in California using this pathway should research specific CSU campuses — their programs, outcomes, and campus cultures — rather than treating CSU admission as a generic credential.
Potential Benefits
  • Reduces access barriers for first-generation and low-income students who don't apply despite being qualified
  • Addresses CSU enrollment declines that threaten program and financial stability
  • Provides certainty and reduces application anxiety for qualified students
Key Concerns
  • Automatic admission may not reflect intentional fit — students need to actively research campus options
  • Law does not guarantee admission to high-demand programs (nursing, engineering, business) at specific campuses
  • Access without intentional placement can result in poor fit and lower completion rates
Key Articles This Week
  • CBS 8 San Diego — 'New California Law: Automatic Admission into CSU' (Jan. 1, 2026)
  • CalMatters — 'Even More Cal State Campuses Will Automatically Admit Eligible Students' (Jan. 2026)
  • California Education Policy Center — SB 640 Analysis
✓ Action Checklist
  • California students: check your eligibility status on CaliforniaColleges.edu for automatic CSU admission
  • Research specific CSU campuses independently — automatic admission to the system doesn't tell you which campus is the best fit
  • Understand that high-demand programs (nursing, engineering) may still have additional selective requirements
→ Action Steps
  1. Visit each CSU campus website to research specific programs, outcomes, and campus culture before relying on automatic admission
  2. Use the CSU campus comparison tool to identify which campus best matches your student's academic and career goals
  3. Contact the admissions office at your preferred campus to understand any program-specific requirements beyond system-level admission
❓ Questions to Ask
  • 'Does my automatic CSU admission apply to all programs at all campuses, or are there additional requirements for specific programs?'
  • 'What is the student success and graduation rate at the specific campus I'm considering?'
  • 'How does your campus support first-generation students in making intentional program and career choices?'

Guidance Counselor Corner

What Every Counselor Should Know and Share This Week

This week's six stories offer counselors a clear theme: the financial architecture of American higher education is being restructured in real time, and families are making decisions based on outdated assumptions. The endowment tax impact on elite institutions is concrete and significant — encourage families to ask explicit questions about aid stability at schools in the 4% and 8% tax tiers.

The Grad PLUS elimination story is one counselors should lead on, not react to. Families with graduate or professional school aspirations need to model the new borrowing math now — not in four years when their students are applying. The most important undergraduate choice for a pre-med student may now include 'does this school have a medical school with strong institutional aid for its own alumni?'

The California automatic admission story is an access tool that creates a counseling opportunity: help students use the access pathway intentionally. The law removes barriers but not the need for fit-based research. Help students understand which CSU campus — not just which system — is the right match.
